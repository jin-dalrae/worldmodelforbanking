The 30-second version

~90 words

For a hallway, a judge walking past, or the answer to "so what did you build?"

A bank cuts someone's credit limit, then waits six months to find out whether that was the right call. And it only ever learns about the choice it made — there is no recording of the road not taken.

So we built a simulator of the household side of a bank. Twelve hundred synthetic families, real cash-flow mechanics, eighteen months of their lives faster than you can move a slider.

We asked it what every credit committee asks in a downturn: what if we cut this group's credit line by twenty percent?

Defaults barely moved. The people who were going to default still did — just on a smaller line. But the bank lost a hundred and thirty thousand dollars in revenue and twenty-one more customers walked away.

The cut squeezed households, and it didn't even pay for itself. Nobody could have known that without running it.

The two-minute version

~300 words

Add the "why doesn't this already exist" beat and the ask. Use when you have a slide or two, or none at all.

Banks are very good at predicting who is risky. They are almost incapable of predicting what happens if they act.

A credit score ranks people at a moment in time. It is a snapshot, not a movie. When a bank actually changes something — a limit, a rate, a hardship offer — the consequences arrive months later, tangled up with the economy, and they only ever see the branch of history they chose.

The honest way to find out is to try it on real customers and count the damage. That is what champion-challenger testing is: buying information with other people's money. So the boldest policies never get tested, and the ones that do get tested get tested on people.

[If you have the deck, this is where slide 3 lives — five mature tools, none of which simulates a household.]

We built the missing piece: a world model of retail banking. Not a better score — a generative simulator of households. Paychecks land, rent goes out, a card gets declined at a grocery store, someone slides into delinquency over ninety days.

The design choice that matters: the accounting is hard-coded and only the behavior is learned. Interest, minimum payments, balances — those obey the arithmetic exactly, because a simulator whose balances drift is one a risk committee throws out on sight. The learning sits on top, in how people behave.

It runs today. Twelve hundred households, eighteen months, two futures side by side that share the same random shocks — same people, same luck, only the policy differs.

And the first real answer it gave us was not the one we expected. A twenty percent line cut barely moved defaults, saved sixty-nine thousand in write-offs, and cost a hundred and thirty thousand in lost revenue. Minus forty-nine dollars per household. The intervention destroys value.

That is the product: the ability to find that out on a Tuesday afternoon instead of eighteen months and several thousand customers later.

The full run

Open on the idea, not the credentials

"We built a video-game version of a bank's customers." Then immediately make it concrete: press play, watch eighteen months of their money, go back, change one thing the bank does, play it again. 02

The problem, then the question, out loud

0:40 – 1:40

Three beats: the answer arrives six months late; the only way to learn is to experiment on customers; and the road not taken was never recorded. Then read the quote on the slide word for word and pause. It is a completely ordinary question and nobody in the building can answer it.

Trap: do not rush the pause. The silence after the question is doing work.

03

Why the existing stack can't

1:40 – 2:20

Go across the table fast — scores are snapshots, live tests are slow and costly, capital models shock a portfolio number and never touch a household, stress models are too aggregated, and the trading-desk simulators are built for prices that move every millisecond. Close with: "we are not trying to build a better credit score."

Trap: do not read five rows aloud. Name two, gesture at the rest.

The loop

The one engineering idea

06

What a score cannot see

3:50 – 4:30

Walk the tape: paycheck, rent, groceries — then an $890 hospital bill and, the next day, a cash advance. That is somebody out of options, and it is visible weeks before a score would blink. Then the decline cascade: most people just pull out a different card, and that is the expensive branch, because from then on that is the card they reach for. You did not lose a sale, you lost the habit.

Trap: cuttable if you are behind. The demo earns more than this slide does.

07

Levers and the scoreboard

4:30 – 5:00

Four sliders you can actually drag, and a reward measured in dollars rather than a score. Then the constraint line, which is the part worth caring about: tell a system only to win and it finds ugly ways to win. So the limits go in at the start, and the model's internal read of a person is never allowed to be the reason someone is turned down.

Run it, then deliver the result as a reversal — defaults barely move, write-offs fall, revenue falls further, customers leave, and the cohort ends up forty-nine dollars per household worse off. Close the beat with: "there was no way to know that without running it."

Who wants it

Roadmap, risks, close

7:00 – 8:00

Stage one is done — they just watched it. Then be honest, because it buys more credibility than it costs: the evidence on line cuts is thin, the risk committee may reject the learned layer, and somebody will want to wire this into live decisions before it is ready. Say what you did about each. Then close on the last line: today a bank learns from money it has already lost; we have shown it can learn first.

Trap: do not end on the roadmap. End on the sentence.

The one-line arithmetic

Saves $68.8k in credit losses. Gives up $130.4k in revenue — $111.5k of margin plus $18.9k of interchange. Net: the cut loses money.

Why deposits go up

Households with a thinner line hold a bigger cash buffer. It looks like a win, and it is the smallest one on the board.

The good case, if the room asks for it

Do not lead with this — it lands far harder after the number. But have it ready, and keep it concrete.

Credit tightening in a downturn falls hardest on the households with the least buffer, and today it is done largely on instinct, then validated after the damage has already landed on real people.

What this changes is the order of events. The harm becomes visible before it is inflicted, in the same units the bank already uses to make the decision.

And in the case we ran, the humane answer and the profitable answer are the same answer. Squeezing people who are already thin does not prevent the defaults — it just moves the same losses onto a smaller line while the customer quietly leaves. That alignment is the argument. You are not asking a bank to be generous; you are showing it that the harsher policy is also the expensive one.

Three things we built in rather than bolted on: it is decision support, with no connection to any system that changes an individual's limit; the model's internal read of a person is never a permitted reason to decline anyone; and the synthetic data means fraud and fairness research can happen without exposing a single real person's financial records.

Hard questions

The eight you should expect. The first two decide whether a serious person keeps listening.

How do you know the simulation is right?
Say the honest thing first: right now we have validated the mechanism, not the magnitudes. The accounting is exact and tested to the cent, the behavioral rules are hand-calibrated and written down, and the direction of the headline effect is asserted by a check that ships with the code. What we have not done is calibrate against a real portfolio — that is the first thing that happens with a design partner, and until it does, the honest output is a range and a direction, not a forecast. That is also why nothing ships straight from simulation to production.
Isn't this just a Monte Carlo credit model with extra steps?
No, and the difference is the whole point. A Monte Carlo credit model samples from a calibrated loss distribution — independent draws against a portfolio. It has no household in it, so it cannot tell you where the spending went when a limit got cut, or that the customer moved top-of-wallet to a competitor, or that deposits rose because people got scared. Every number on the right-hand side of that table except charge-offs is invisible to a PD/LGD engine.
Why not just train one big model on transaction data?
Because a next-event model will happily generate a balance that ignores the credit limit, or statement math that does not add up, or a default with no ninety-day lag in front of it. When balances drift, model risk rejects the system, and they are right to. So the ledger stays deterministic and the learned component sits on top as a correction to behavior. That is not a compromise; it is the thing that makes it approvable.
What stops a bank using this to justify harming customers?
Structurally: there is no write path to account management, no production status in the API, and it is in the contract. Practically: the constraints live inside the optimizer rather than in a review afterwards, and latent embeddings are barred from being adverse-action reasons. And in the case we just showed, the tool argued against the harmful policy on the bank's own terms — which is worth more than any promise we could make about intent.
Does this replace CECL or CCAR models?
No, and we will not let it be sold that way. Those are regulated production models with filing obligations. This is decision support that may inform an overlay. There is no supervisory export format in v1, deliberately.
Where does the data come from, and does it leave the bank?
Today it is fully synthetic — the demo generates its own population from a seed, so there is nothing to leak. In deployment the model runs inside the customer's own environment on tokenized event streams, and raw data never leaves. The synthetic export exists so vendors and internal teams can work on realistic data without an access review.
Twelve hundred households is not a portfolio.
Correct — it is a proof of mechanism that runs in a browser tab with no server. The design target is ten thousand accounts interactively and a million overnight; getting there is exactly what the next stage is for. The reason to show you twelve hundred is that you can watch it re-run while you move a slider.
What's the business model?
Be straight: pricing is still an open question in the PRD. The shape is a platform deployed per-institution inside their environment, and the first commitment we want is not a cheque but one card portfolio and one risk team willing to point it at their own data.
