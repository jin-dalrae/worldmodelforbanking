World Model for Banking

Simulate the household, not the order book.

Capital markets have simulators. Retail banking does not — because retail banking has to simulate people. Paychecks, rent, a card declined at the grocery store, the slow slide into delinquency. This one generates the event-level life of a household under a policy the bank has not tried yet, so the experiment runs in a simulator instead of on customers.

What it isA governed, generative simulator of the household financial lifecycle
DeploymentRuns inside the bank's own VPC — no raw data leaves

02The problemAn experiment graded 180 days late

Every credit policy decision is an experiment run on real people.

Delayed truth

Defaults surface 90–180 days after the decision that caused them. Churn is slower still. By the time the outcome lands, the macro conditions that produced it are gone.

Costly exploration

Champion/challenger tests buy information with real losses and real customers. The policies worth knowing about — line cuts in a downturn — are the ones that never get tested.

No counterfactual

A backtest can only score what happened. It cannot say what a customer would have done under a different limit, because that spending was never recorded.

03PositioningWhy the existing stack can't answer it

Six mature tools. None of them simulates a household.

Scorecards

Macro / DSGE simulatorsCoherent paths for GDP, rates and unemployment.There is no person and no merchant in the model. It cannot say which card gets reached for, or whether a reward is used as intended.
Capital-markets world modelsStochastic prices, order books, market impact.Wrong state, wrong agents, wrong clock. Prices are fast and continuous; card events are discrete, irregular and habit-driven.

The gap is not a better PD model. The gap is a calibrated generative simulator of the financial lifecycle, governed well enough that model risk will accept it as decision support.

05Architecture4 of 5 layers running today

Accounting is enforced. Only behavior is learned.

LayerRoleWhy it is built this way
L0  LedgerrunningDeterministic posting engine — balances, cycles, interest, minimum payment, aging.Non-negotiable. Learn the ledger and balances drift, and model risk rejects it.
L1  HouseholdsrunningCalibrated microsimulation — income, essential burn, liquidity-constrained payment, substitution.Interpretable and stress-stable. Runs a million accounts on CPU, and answers the MVP question alone.
L2  ResidualdesignedEvent transformer correcting spend intensity, payment propensity, churn hazard, merchant choice.Captures the habit the structural layer misses. Shrunk toward zero, and always decoded through L0.
L3  ShocksrunningMacro to regional to household — unemployment, CPI, medical and job-loss mixtures.Keeps exogenous climate separate from the policy being tested.
L4  PolicyrunningDeclarative issuer rules in v1; constrained RL later, as a consumer of the environment.The learner never sits inside the kernel. That boundary is what keeps v1 reviewable.
Runtime, one simulated month
structural rate×learned correction→sample events→post to ledger→close

Teal is running. The correction term is currently the identity.

Why not one giant transformer

A next-event model will not enforce a credit limit, statement math, or a 90-day default lag. It earns its place as a correction term and a synthetic decoder — never as the accounting.

Two customers can hold the same credit score while one of them is three weeks from empty. The score cannot separate them. The stream can — cash buffer, burn rate, and where this card sits in the wallet are read straight off the events.

02 — Where the money goes when the card says no
limit reached—→ authorization declined—→ competitor card cart abandoned pay-later at checkout retry same card

Each branch writes a different future. The competitor branch re-ranks the wallet permanently and costs interchange for months; abandonment keeps the relationship but suppresses spend. A model that only predicts default sees neither.

07Action spaceLevers, objective, constraints

What the bank may pull, and what counts as winning.

All four levers are live sliders in the demo: credit line, APR, cashback, and a hardship program. The reward below is the one the engine actually computes, in dollars.

Line management running

Limit increases and cuts, and freezes with a duration. The cuts matter most: they are the policies that are effectively untestable in production today.

Timing and hardship running

Proactive alerts, balance-transfer offers, restructuring — placed before delinquency, where the simulation says the household is weeks from a shortfall.

Pricing and rewards running

APR moves, category multipliers, authorization tightness. Measured on risk-adjusted lifetime value, not thirty-day spend lift.

R_t = NIM + interchange − charge-offs − churn cost − cashback // monthly, discounted 12% annual NIM = interest − (fed funds + 50bps) × receivables interchange = 1.8% of card spend recovery at default = 18% subject to fair-lending constraints credit appetite max line change per period operational feasibility

The constraint line is not decoration. Optimize the reward alone and the policy finds the same shortcuts a bad underwriter would. Constraints live inside the optimizer, with the trade-off curve written down — and latent embeddings are never used as a reason for declining anyone.

09Ask the modelFor strategists and marketers

The question a macro model cannot take.

The question

“A co-brand card with triple points at theme parks — will people use it there, or just farm airline miles?”

Answered from 1,200 simulated households
5% genuinely new 47% moved 48% gamed

Pay 5% on $1.39M of volume to earn $722 of interchange on genuinely new spend. Net −$68.6k. The people who stack miles are the ones who take the offer.

Why the answer can be trusted
